---
title: The Twyman-Stokes Teammate of the Year Award
date: 2025-07-31
tags: Basketball
---

In 2022, the National Basketball Association (NBA) added new awards, as well as renamed their awards after iconic players. A handful of these star basketball players' names are probably familiar for even non-basketball fans, such as the Michael Jordan award for the league's Most Valuable Player (MVP) and the Kobe Bryant award for the MVP of the All-Star game. The vast majority of these awards are all about the on-court success and achievements of these basketball players, but a few of them extend off the court. One such award is the Twyman-Stokes Teammate of the Year award, and its origin is my favorite story in all of basketball.

Maurice Stokes was born in 1933 near Pittsburgh, Pennsylvania. He was a loved and highly respected individual amongst his friends and peers and that theme would continue into his NBA career. He stood out during his college years at St. Francis University, becoming one of the most sought-after prospects for the 1955 NBA Draft. At just 22 years old, Stokes was selected with the second overall pick by the Rochester Royals.

At 6'7" and 232 pounds, Stokes was a freakishly quick and strong power forward with extraordinary athleticism and exceptional basketball skills to match. In an era where big men typically wouldn't dribble the ball on the **fastbreak** (*quickly running up the court as the offense after getting the ball back from playing defense via a steal, block, rebound, or just quickly passing the ball in after a made shot*), Stokes was the exception as he would frequently grab the rebound and take the ball coast-to-coast himself. Maurice Stokes was also a very intelligent basketball player who had incredible court vision, which complemented his ability to bring the ball up during the fastbreak. Honestly, the best comparison I can make to his build and skill set is that Maurice Stokes was the LeBron James of the 1950s, and it really isn't that far-fetched to make this claim as modern fans might think.

Stokes was dominant from the get-go. In his debut game against the New York Knicks, he had a statline of 32 points, 20 rebounds, and 8 assists while shooting 61% from the field. His rare ability to score, rebound, and facilitate at elite levels made him one of the earliest true **triple-double** (*tallying at least 10 in three statistical categories between points/rebounds/assists/steals/blocks, most commonly done with points/rebounds/assists*) threats. At one point, he recorded a triple-double in four consecutive games which is a feat that only 8 other players have accomplished in NBA history (Russell Westbrook - 11, Wilt Chamberlain - 9, Oscar Robertson - 7, Michael Jordan - 7, Luka Doncic - 7, Nikola Jokic - 5, Magic Johnson - 4, LeBron James - 4). Just one month after his debut, Stokes dropped 31 points, 27 rebounds, and 6 assists in a one-point victory against the Boston Celtics, one of the stronger teams at the time. Another month later, he had one of the greatest performances ever by a rookie in any sport against the defending champion Syracuse Nationals with a jaw-dropping performance: 26 points, 38 rebounds, and 12 assists on 61% shooting. His efforts led the Royals to a 102–93 upset win.

By the end of his rookie season, Stokes was named Rookie of the Year. He averaged 16.8 points, 16.3 rebounds, and 4.9 assists, even leading the league in rebounds as a first-year player. His dominance continued as he was named an All-Star and selected as an All-NBA player in each of his three seasons. In his third year, he ranked second in rebounds and third in assists. The only other player who has ever accomplished that feat was the legendary Wilt Chamberlain (although Nikola Jokic came close in the 2024-2025 season). I should emphasize that he wasn't just a good rebounder but at the mere height of 6'7" (which is relatively short for players at his position) he was one of the few greatest rebounders of all time. Stokes averaged 17.3 rebounds per game for his career, which is third all-time behind only Wilt Chamberlain and Bill Russell.

In his third season, Stokes led his Royals to the playoffs, but unfortunately it would be the only postseason appearance of his career. On March 12, 1958, during the final regular season game, Stokes leaped high into the air and went over the back of the opponent and landed directly on his head. For a while, he was unconscious, but eventually he was able to regain consciousness with smelling salts. Despite the horrific fall, he returned to the game and finished the contest with 24 points and 19 rebounds and a seven-point victory over the Lakers.

Three days later was his first NBA playoff game and little did he know that it was the last game of his NBA career. On the night, he had 12 points and grabbed 15 rebounds in a loss to the Detroit Pistons. After the game, the Royals went on their flight back to Cincinnati for game two of the series, but during the flight Maurice suddenly collapsed and began to have a seizure. Upon landing, he was rushed to the hospital, where he slipped into a coma. After being in a coma for a day, he woke up to discover that he was completely paralyzed — only able to use his brain and look around with his eyes. Doctors confirmed that the fall had caused a traumatic brain injury.

Of course, this completely changed Maurice Stokes' life as he not only lost function and movement but there were now tremendous medical bills to pay that he couldn't afford. Maurice didn't have much of a support system and didn't have very many loved ones to take care of him. This was something that deeply affected his teammates emotionally. One of those teammates throughout all three of his years in the NBA was the small forward and his dear friend Jack Twyman who was the only teammate who lived near Stokes. Jack was only 23 years old at this point and had recently got married and started his own family. After speaking with his wife about the matter they came to a major decision. Out of love and compassion for his teammate, Jack decided to become a caretaker and the legal guardian of Maurice, accepting all financial responsibility and all responsibilities for his well-being. Jack was now there to support his teammate and his friend as Stokes fought hard to recover some of his physical abilities.

Despite being paralyzed, Maurice remained fiercely determined and channeled the same drive he had on the court into his rehab. Maurice slowly started to regain tiny bits of functionality in his body. During this period, the bond and friendship between Maurice and Jack grew stronger than ever as the two leaned upon each other for support. In 1969, more than a decade after Stokes' paralyzing fall, St. Francis University reached out to Twyman. They had built a new beautiful gymnasium and wanted to honor their former star player by naming the gym the Maurice Stokes Fieldhouse. As a surprise for Maurice's birthday, Jack and his family held an event where the president and athletic director of the college announced the name of the gym to Maurice who was unaware of the decision up until that point. Overwhelmed with gratitude, Maurice broke down in tears.

Back then, NBA salaries were nowhere near what they are today, so even a star player like Jack Twyman needed financial aid to support Maurice. So raising money for Maurice's care became a priority. A man by the name of Milton Kutsher was a business owner of a country club in upstate New York and was a massive basketball fan. Milton was aware of the situation between Maurice Stokes and Jack Twyman. Kutsher reached out to Twyman and suggested that the country club could host a basketball game to raise funds for Maurice's medical bills. Milton said that he would cover all of the expenses for the event — all Jack had to do was recruit the players, at minimum 10 players for a game to commence.

Remarkably, the game didn't just attract 10 players. Over 75 of the NBA's best players showed up, all at their own expense, which was a testament to Stokes being such a beloved person. These included legends like Wilt Chamberlain, Bill Russell, and Dolph Schayes. The event became an annual tradition, with greats like Oscar Robertson, Kareem Abdul-Jabbar, and more consistently appearing to support the cause. After some time, once some of his physical abilities just slightly returned to him, Maurice was even able to attend his own event and experience once again the love that the league had for him.

Eventually on April 6, 1970, Maurice Stokes passed away from a heart attack at the young age of 36. After his passing, the event continued on in the form of a basketball charity game for many years, preserving his legacy and touching the lives of many after him.

As you can see, throughout this story not only was Stokes a powerful force within the league with his basketball abilities, but he was also a powerful force in the hearts of many as his story is one of perseverance and friendship. In terms of basketball, Jack Twyman is on record saying that if the accident never happened, Stokes was easily on his way to becoming a top 50 basketball player of all time (and in my opinion had top 10 potential). Obviously we will never know for sure, but what we do know is that in the short time that Stokes was out there competing, he was one of the best players to ever step on the court.

In a time where racial tensions ran rampant, Jack Twyman's gesture is even more amazing to me. Twyman's and Stokes' friendship represents the beauty of basketball in its best form and the bonds that are created from playing with teammates.
