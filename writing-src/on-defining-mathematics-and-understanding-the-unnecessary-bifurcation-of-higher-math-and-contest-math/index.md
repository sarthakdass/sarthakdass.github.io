I will put forward a key perspective that I have matured to over time, which contradicts the vast majority of my superiors. The proposition I will present is that the rift between Olympiad mathematics and higher mathematics is overly strained and is an actively harmful viewpoint to reiterate (See [Terrence Tao](https://terrytao.wordpress.com/career-advice/advice-on-mathematics-competitions/), [Cathy O'Neil](https://mathbabe.org/2011/07/17/math-contests-kind-of-suck/), [Moor Xu](https://annoyingpi.wordpress.com/2010/05/22/contest-math/)). I do understand and agree with the claim that research and contest math is different; I am just against the incessant historical need to push this adage. Some will go as far as to inculcate in aspiring pupils that math contests actively impede the ability to produce novel ideas, as math contests are about uncovering techniques in a limited amount of time for solved problems, whereas math research focuses upon the ability to formulate conclusions about the unsolved.

## The Spelling Bee

> "Quickness is helpful in mathematics, but it is only one of the qualities which is helpful. For people who do not become mathematicians, the skills of contest math are probably even less relevant. These contests are a bit like spelling bees. There is some connection between good spelling and good writing, but the winner of the state spelling bee does not necessarily have the talent to become a good writer, and some fine writers are not good spellers. If there was a popular confusion between good spelling and good writing, many potential writers would be unnecessarily discouraged."
>
> **- Fields Medalist William Thurston**

At this point, I'm sort of "speedrunning" how many great mathematicians can I disagree with, and you'd be right that probably I'm out of my mind to express these opinions in the first place. The first observation is that Thurston's opinions make sense in an ideal world: if the K-12 school system was able to provide an approachable and accurate sense of academic mathematical writing, no longer would we need math contests to give students that extra push to pursue mathematics. The problem is that, simply put, higher math is *really hard*. I genuinely believe that the American Mathematics Competitions do a great job in terms of providing an approachable level for high schoolers as to what mathematicians do, even if it is not remotely accurate.

Comparing Olympiad mathematics to the Spelling Bee is laughably inane to me. Surely, we can recognize how disingenuous this stance is to compare math contests to rote memorization of dictionary words and spelling correctly. I'll get into this topic later in terms of how contest math is viewed (and how I romanticize it should be viewed), but Olympiads provide a great stepping stool to introduce higher level topics which mathematicians could devote their entire life and career to. Additionally, I wholeheartedly believe the best performers are not the ones who have memorized the most "tricks" but are the ones who truly have grasped the mathematical theory at a very high level.

## Pickleball, Tennis, and Distance Running

For the past 6 months, a running joke in one of my friend groups is the validity of the sport of pickleball and its true difficulty (of which I am of the stance that pickleball is a valid sport and does require significant skill). One of them in particular who is one of my best friends, I shall call M, will make it a specific point to look up the "best pickleball rallies" or the "top 10 pickleball points" and find great amusement in the lack of movement in pickleball in contrast to the raw athleticism that exists in tennis. Bear in mind that this is a popular sentiment not just extracted from one friend, but so many people are of this opinion.

> "Pickleball just isn't the same as tennis."
>
> **- M**

My reaction, and what I would expect from most people who are reasonable, is to think "okay obviously, and so what?" This is a factual statement, but completely irrelevant; pickleball and tennis are not even remotely close to identical, despite the surface-level similarities. But it is also highly likely that a world-class tennis player will be significantly better than average at pickleball and vice versa, but there is no reason to expect a skillful player of one to **instantaneously** be top-tier in the other.

But maybe pickleball and tennis are not close enough of an analogy according to you.

Here's another analogy to consider that might seem even more similar and relevant, which I was introduced to in conversation with mathematician [Maria Monks Gillespie](https://mathematicalgemstones.com/maria/). Most people would classify both a 5K and a marathon as long-distance running. I think it's also not a terrible analogy to represent contest math as the 5K and mathematical research as the marathon. As the 5K is more than 8 times shorter than a marathon, I would be inclined to agree that the marathon requires more priority training for skills pertaining to longer distances such as endurance, patience, pacing and diligence whereas the 5K involves more speed, strength, competitive edge, and strategy.

Could you really argue in good faith that these events are totally uncorrelated? Does not sound ridiculous to say the 5K event is the "spelling bee" of long-distance running and thus has no bearing on predicting marathon success? The best 5K runners would excel in the marathon (especially after some endurance training) and the best marathon runners would smoke most 5K runners in the world (also after some targeted training). Would they instantly be the very best in the other distance? Most certainly not, but they would still be highly proficient! In similar vein, I have seen evidence that the best Olympiad students have the ability to produce good research and I have no doubt whatsoever that the best mathematicians in the world would perform very well on the Olympiads.

That being said, of course there are countless examples of people who never ran long distance or 5Ks in their youth but can start their running in young adulthood to achieve a very respectable time in a marathon; I do not disagree with this. But I simply cannot agree with the proposition that contest math provides no benefit for higher math endeavors.

## Understanding from Overhearing

Consider the following conversation I overheard about one of my smartest friends I have known in competition math, whom I will denote as K.

> Person A: "Wow, **K** solves like every problem we get, what can't she solve?"
>
> Person B: "Meh, probably any *real math problem* which is worth asking. We'll see what she's going to do over time, but contest math is *silly and unhelpful* for actual math research."

This is when it all clicked.

Let's attempt to dissect this. Why do people, including math superiors, chastise those who pursue and excel at contest math? And why am I *so bothered* about this?

I spent so much time on the pickleball vs. tennis and distance-running analogies to make this particular comparison: people will make the same obvious claim that the things being compared are not the same. Whenever the topic gets brought up about some student medaling in the IMO, there's always someone who leaps upon the opportunity to excitedly point out the particular maxim about contest math vs. research. I have acknowledged already that I do literally believe it is a statement of truth, but here's the problem: **there's an underlying negative implication of performing well in contests and not being good in research.**

I counter by asking what's so wrong about that? The answer to this question lies within the answer to another question, which is "what is math?"

I believe there's been a rather nasty trick that's been played in the scope of this debate, and unfortunately, we've allowed the wool to be pulled over our eyes. The way to unveil this trick is by [tabooing the word "math"](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/WBdvyyHLdxZSAMmoz/taboo-your-words). Here are a few things the word "math" could be referring to, among others:

- Learning subjects such as calculus, analysis, probability, algebra, etc.
- Engaging in quantitative thinking (logic, reasoning, mental math)
- Training for Olympiads and contests
- Working on open problems and conjectures (known as "research")

The trick is that the research community has made itself synonymous with "math" and thus owned the term for themselves. And in doing so, the second bullet point of "math contests" is left strictly for the Olympiad community. Let's take a look at this sentence to demonstrate my point.

> "Math contests should aim to be relevant to math"

This statement seems innocuous. But say we taboo the word "math"; this is probably the natural resulting sentence:

> "Olympiads should aim to be relevant to research."

Suddenly, *this feels wrong*. Due to the term "math" being a semantic substring of "math contests", now suddenly it's reasonable to claim Olympiads are **subsidiary** to research. The premise is that universally people do agree that research and contests are not the same, but it is fallacious to then claim math contests are "silly and unhelpful."

I think that the concept of math contests provides students with a direction of intellectual interest to work on, which in and of itself does a lot of good, independent of any value to future math research. The training and preparation process for a contest teaches students invaluable life lessons: to constantly work hard even in the face of pressure and possible failure, and what it takes for someone to be really skilled at something after dedicated years of hard work. As I have also alluded to in a past post, it's a gateway to a wonderful community of people who despite the competitive nature, are ultimately very supportive. The problem-solving ability you attain from this pursuit is [ubiquitously helpful in life](https://www.quora.com/Does-problem-solving-ability-in-math-such-as-being-able-to-solve-IMO-problems-help-you-in-other-aspects-of-life). So yes, my point is indeed that the biggest benefits of contest math have absolutely nothing to do with math.

## My Personal Journey

When I was younger, I primarily viewed Olympiad mathematics as a pure venture of problem solving, as that was all which I had known and had been told. But this couldn't be further from the truth in my opinion. Perhaps this is a bit bold of a take, but I am a strong believer that for a surprisingly high percentage of Olympiad problems, solving them isn't *too challenging* if you truly understand theory to a deep level (*this is the incredibly nontrivial part!*).

One description that I've commonly heard to encapsulate training for the Olympiads is that you're acquiring tools and building up your toolkit to learn to apply these tools. This is usually my go-to explanation for describing contest math in a single sentence to those who are passively curious about it being one of my hobbies. The unfortunate misnomer which occurs from this description is that people (including me initially!) tend to think that when solving problems, your goal is to recognize which tools to use and how to creatively use them. When I reflect on my better days of solving problems, this description is woefully inaccurate. Sure, on my bad days I definitely had moments where I just had to admit defeat to math and try random spammy things which sometimes still worked and more often than not failed on the spot. There were definitely instances in which I luckily recognized something, but these were few and far between. In my somewhat romanticized view of how I'd like to think I approached math problems, **I would think about theories instead of theorems** and attempt to understand the relation of the problem to the theories, and once I understood that relation well enough, the solution was clear to me.

To offer an example of what I mean: in many ways, number theory reflects the study of polynomials. Intuitively, one may realize that taking mod a prime corresponds to taking mod $(x-a)$, or plugging in $a$, etc. rather than memorize "The Remainder Theorem". Thus, I would often approach number theory problems by the problem's relation to the polynomial perspective and the "authentic" number theory perspective, i.e. things like the Frobenius automorphism. For some problems, I'm able to see that the key observation of the problem lies within the Frobenius automorphism, and that it becomes a matter of attempting to manipulate the problem until its clear exactly how it's a Frobenius automorphism problem.

I truly believe the hardest part of this extensive process for conceptual and problem-solving mastery is the understanding of the theory. And I find that this process does translate really well to higher math; this is strikingly similar to what I do whenever I learn a new theorem/concept. Of course, there are several differences. For one, it's nearly impossible to successfully do this at the start. Instead, you just have to relentlessly never stop trying to understand the theorems at first, and once you reach the end, you will *probably* be able to gain a sufficient level of understanding (*and then you can understand the later theorems, and so on...*).

I would also hear so many people purport that (higher) math is *something* and that has to be *done in so-and-so way* (especially in the context of dismissing Olympiad math), when it really doesn't need to… These words are dangerously non-inclusive and inducive of mathematical fearmongering. If an artist were to tell you that there is only one rote way to learn to paint masterpieces, wouldn't you scoff and call such a viewpoint hogwash? Mathematics is so often regarded as one of the sciences (as it should be!) that it's forgotten that it's one of the **purest art forms** mankind has endeavored! I honestly think so many approaches are possible for a specific problem or concept, but if you tread on the road not taken, you'll need to spend a long time getting nowhere before you figure out how to make your approach work. That being said, struggle is one of the best forms of productivity.

This is more so a strong personal opinion to close, but here's my problem with higher math: **It's so hard to appreciate the "true" beauty of many subjects of higher mathematics before a very select few courses**. One of these classes is most certainly complex analysis (*note that in theory it is possible to study complex analysis without much more knowledge than multivariable calculus!*). There are even some absolutely incredible gems of ideas buried deeply behind linear algebra, but their existence is dubious because the subject appears so terrible at first glance (*although to be fair, this I directly blame poor pedagogy rather than the intrinsic nature of the subject; case in point: [3Blue1Brown's Essence of Linear Algebra](https://www.youtube.com/playlist?list=PLZHQObOWTQDPD3MizzM2xVFitgF8hE_ab)*). There are definitely individual moments of wonder and beauty I still recognize in my classes, but perhaps the first time I could say I truly loved the entirety of math was when I started reading **algebraic geometry**[^1]. Everything which I had monotonously glossed over suddenly seemed so much more interesting (*and also so much easier to understand*). Granted, my initial reading of AG was not by any means one of understanding, but rather I was wonderstruck by the sheer beauty of the subject. What this allowed me to do was understand the development of the relevant fundamental higher mathematics.

[^1]: *I am using Hartshorne's AG but I also highly recommend Ravi Vakil's FOAG notes.*

## The Culture Issue and a Hopeful Closing

The primary reason for this existing divide is due to the culture and environment which pervades each of them. I continuously hear that there are two distinct groups of math enthusiasts, those who do the virtuous thing of going into math research and those who renege the authentic spirit of mathematics and move on to finance, software, and various applications. Yet again, there's this unspoken message which belittles those who choose to not pursue math research. There's a stigma placed upon these students in the sense that all they can do well is take tests and not necessarily be "actually good at math."

This is so harmful and creates such a problem out of a non-issue because when did anyone ever claim that the purpose of contest math was to produce better math researchers? The funny thing is that despite all the preaching that contest math and research are so different, so many Olympiad students do end up going into research. Of course, I do think that the case could be made that it's just a natural direction to head towards since for these students, it's the equivalent of entering a door with a neon "math" sign flashing above it, without realizing it's a totally different topic.

There are countless reasons to critique the contest math community and Olympiads in general (which could be yet another blog post on its own); however, "Olympiads are math research" is simply not one of those valid criticisms. Just to name a big one, the contest math community itself is undeniably quite elitist and cliques into a hive which walls themselves off from outsiders.

But here's the thing: I beg that now more than ever, we as a global society desperately need this kind of problem-solving talent across the world to resolve issues rather than for it to be all spent on something like **computing $R(6, 6)$** (*yes, I do feel slightly guilty to bully Ramsey Theory shortly after a dedicated post about it*). In the words of Art of Problem Solving (AoPS) founder Richard Rusczyk at the [2014 Math Prize for Girls](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=TYCxbFad36g):

> ***When people ask me, "am I disappointed when my students don't go off and become mathematicians", my answer is that I'd honestly be very disappointed if they all did. We need people who can think about these complex problems and solve really hard problems they haven't seen before everywhere. It's not just in math, it's not just in the sciences — I mean, what we'd give to get some of them in Congress!***

Academia is a fine and highly respectable career, but for math majors, there's honestly so many other options out there. The research community could potentially look down upon those who stray away from math, but society will always welcome these people with open arms. There have simply been too many instances I have seen my friends (and experienced myself) talk about competition math, only to be met with disinterestedness or maybe even a lecture about the lack of maturity of Olympiads.

> ***Contest math is already inaccessible to over 99% of the people as it is, and then we're supposed to tell those few that get it, that even that isn't real math? While we're at it, why don't we tell [Vi Hart](https://vimeo.com/vihart) to stop making videos because they don't accurately represent math research.***
>
> ***- Steven Karp***
