Happy International Women's Day (and also Women's History Month)!

One of my favorite articles of all time is [*The Egg and the Sperm: How Science has Constructed a Romance Based on Stereotypical Male - Female Roles*](https://web.stanford.edu/~eckert/PDF/Martin1991.pdf) by medical anthropologist Emily Martin in 1991 and it honestly blows my mind every single time that I read it. I just reread the article this week, which is why I have been thinking about it!

**If you have the time, I would rather you read the entire article than any part of my blog post. I truly admire how accessibly it is written and how effectively its arguments are made.**

It talks about the gendered stereotypes that are hidden in the scientific language (and literature) of biology, and in my opinion, it illustrates its point quite effectively in how we describe the act of fertilization. The language that is used in textbooks denotes the sperm as this active, aggressive, ambitious, dynamic swimmer that has to race heroically toward its goal. On the other hand, the egg is this passive, demure, waiting, receiving kind of archetype. If any of you played [The Great Sperm Race](https://www.gameflare.com/online-game/the-great-sperm-race/) game in school like I did for sex education or have heard of [Become](https://store.steampowered.com/app/3710850/Become/), you know what I am talking about! When we read this in a textbook, it *sounds* scientific and due that, most people assume that this is an objective representation of the process, when it's really not.

Science doesn't just describe the world; it really shapes how we think about it. The paper uses Darwinism as an example, which is a scientific construct that has been used socially to justify wealth accumulation and capitalism. This is just one of many examples of how scientific language bleeds into and shapes culture all the time. When we look at how we narrate reproduction, it is absolutely ludicrous. In textbook after textbook, the egg is described like it has any semblance of personality, i.e. *she's the nurturer…she's the receiver…she waits and she shrivels and she dies alone every single month*…and as she gets older, she loses her value unless a healthy young man (or in this case, the sperm) comes along to save her from this desolate fate and give her purpose.

And within the scope of such framing, it exactly mirrors the stereotypes which have been assigned to women. But scientifically, this is simply not true. Fertilization is a mutualistic process in which the egg is biologically active — it releases chemical agents, it changes its outer layer, it plays a role in selecting which sperm actually break through and begin to fuse with the egg. The egg is not at all the damsel in distress that it is portrayed to be.

The language we use *assigns it to that role* and then we prescribe those characteristics onto women in the name of biology.

But this is not biology.

It's just the language that we are *choosing to use* to describe biology. I just feel as if the big underlying point is that a person's eggs are not passive objects waiting for something or someone to happen to them. And thus, to the relevant audience, *you shouldn't be either*!
