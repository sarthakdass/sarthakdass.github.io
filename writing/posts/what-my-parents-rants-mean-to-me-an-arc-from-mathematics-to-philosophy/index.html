What my Parents' Rants Mean to Me; an Arc from Mathematics to Philosophy

November 27, 20259 min readThoughts

At the request of a couple people, I will do my best to make the occasional personal level post, instead of always talking about other things. After all, I suppose this is a blog (which I can admit has been popping off lately, thanks to the break!). I’m not a great writer, and I do often struggle with eloquently writing about who I am and what I believe in, but one of the coolest parts about living life is that I get to try new things and be comfortable embracing discomfort!

Growing up, my parents used to make me do math. A lot of math. This is probably not of much shock to those who know me, but I have noticed over the years how my parents eventually transitioned their sayings to be less about doing math, and more preaching their own philosophies to me.

My parents would reiterate to me, “always question what you see” in the context of math. I think that this advice definitely molded me to become a better mathematician but perhaps a bit more questionable as a person. I would agree that I feel competent with my ability to critically think and approach problems in general, but sometimes in life, not all of the variables are present or known to you. I do think that I applied this advice to my physical experiences to some positive benefit, but at times too literally, which is where things would occasionally go south. In fact, there were instances where I messed up as an elementary schooler by asking too many questions and got myself into trouble for being a smart-aleck. I also was not a very easily trusting person when I was younger, and this definitely made it more difficult to make friends. More often than not, I would decline invitations and keep to myself since I lived under a fear of what-ifs, and enjoyed the safety of the daily routine I lived. I also could not understand why a peer wouldn’t want to be friends with me in middle school when I criticized and questioned every silly mistake they made on their assignment. After all, didn’t I help them correct their wrong answers to get a good grade? Wasn’t I doing them a favor? I was merely questioning what I saw on their homework!

Later on in life, my parents began telling me, “question why you see what you see.” This became more applicable in terms of not defaulting to overly quick judgment, developing more empathy, and taking life less seriously sometimes. I have been noticing my improvement in this category when I attend university classes and interact with various students since there is a diversity of students from all walks of life. Quite often, some of them will make blanket statements, and I have to check myself and try to understand where they are coming from before instantly launching into arguments. Some students are also more hot-tempered or easily frustrated and I do believe my upbringing and constant practice of applying my parents’ advice helps in communicating with these various students. Additionally, I believe this piece of advice also reflects my parents’ growth: note the lack of absolutes, as well as the maturity of this advice over the other. This piece of advice is my fundamental base for critical thinking.

What would I have improved upon earlier if I could? I would definitely say that at some point, I needed someone to tell me that it can be just as wise to shut up and say nothing (and listen), instead of speaking for the sole purpose of just saying something. Maybe, there are instances where questioning isn’t good and it is better to simply accept. I’ve had friends who would tell little white lies which were honestly inconsequential, and I would be the one to logically think about these statements and uncover contradictions (and unfortunately at times, call them out, leading to very awkward situations) instead of just shutting my brain off and accepting people for what they are and how they wish to be seen. We’re all a little insecure inside and we all just want to be loved. I wish I understood this better, and this is an area that my parents never addressed during my upbringing.

Another big preaching of theirs was regarding how it’s important to not just do “good”, but more importantly, “to generally want to do good things for other people.” This is partly why my parents are religious; because they believe the Hindu stories/fables teach good life lessons. Whether they actually happened is more or less irrelevant to them, but their big idea is to value supporting other people over simply helping oneself, which is what Hindu gods did. I personally am of the opinion that religion is a good thing for the individual, as I can see what it means to my parents and how they abide by certain rules, some more obvious than others. I have seen evidence of what it has meant to them and how their lives have been enhanced through religious tradition. As for when it comes to religion for society, then my opinion does get far more nuanced. Although I am personally agnostic, I can recognize the value of these various religious stories, which is why I do get saddened when these quotes and stories are desecrated to make some particular point (intersection of politics and Christianity!)

I think that there was a long period of time between when I first listened to this advice and when I first acted upon it. My initial reasoning was that I didn’t have the time to do these grand gestures of kindness or have the facilities to greatly help people — after all, what could I do to help someone or make their day better? I turned toward humor. I would intentionally try to keep up with specific areas of social media and attempt to be in tune with the latest news so that I could specifically make a joke and know that my friends would laugh, which I viewed as a superficial way of helping people. But then again, shutting up and listening makes a repeat appearance in this blog post as this was a way that I eventually found helped a lot of people far more than either they or I realized. When the world moves so fast, so many people want to get out their thoughts and be heard.

“Every being cries out silently to be read differently”

- Simone Weil

I am going to have to side-track a bit here, because I love Simone Weil. I know this is going to sound very #CanvasDiscussionPostCoded, but this particular quote resonates with me so much. For a quick background, Simone Weil was a very bright mathematician in her younger days, but she lived in the shadow of her brother, renowned mathematician Andre Weil, and all of his male math friends. She ended up straying away from mathematics, and became a philosopher and an activist.

In fact, I must plug a textbook that I absolutely loved from one of the classes I took which investigated social justice and mathematics. Please read it!!! I cannot stress this enough!

Mathematics for Human Flourishing by Francis Su and Christopher Jackson

When I was a child, it was always more important to be nice than smart; if I misbehaved or did something along the lines of not being nice, then I would be told not to do math for a while because “the worst form of intelligence is to have smarts and do bad things with it.” There were definitely specific days where I didn’t really understand the message and would intentionally goof off at home in order to get out of doing 6+ hours of math that day. But as I have gotten more exposure, I can absolutely see what my parents mean. Around me, I have seen countless people make disingenuous claims, refuse to believe facts, and support rhetoric from influential people which can be very hurtful to specific demographics of people. It’s one thing to be uneducated and make ignorant claims, but a whole other matter to be knowledgeable, and display willful ignorance to downplay people, events, structures, etc. while being fully aware of the influence of your opinion.

My parents used to constantly complain about how “the math community is full of un-caring people”, because most of the people in competitive mathematics are too focused on their own goals to give much consideration for each other. I definitely experienced this firsthand after I was told “I didn’t deserve to win” after winning a contest (getting the highest score on the AMC 8 in middle school as a 6th grader) because others claimed they worked harder and thus, were more deserving or that I simply got lucky. And honestly, at the time the words stung, but it didn’t take me too long to realize that my peers didn’t hate me. Rather, they were studying math because they genuinely enjoyed it, but certainly could have been more graceful. In a hyper-competitive environment, I can warrant that it is fundamentally tough to be caring because you are literally competing against your best friends. But doing math doesn’t have to be all about the competitiveness!

The thing that surprised me the most when I joined a specific online math community on Discord was how caring everyone was. This is probably my favorite aspect of the math community, and to an even greater extent the Math Olympiad community. Generally, students in the boy-dominated math clubs I attended would spam online or scream in-person in the name of “discourse” which fostered a battleground festooned with spammy middle/high schoolers borderline harassing each other for their own twisted idea of fun (I warrant that this may be a bit of an exaggeration). So, it was really surprising to see people actually using hearts unironically, or unashamedly were willing to be vulnerable to show that they actually cared about each other. I had never seen this level of genuine support in a math community before, and was very pleasantly surprised while simultaneously amazed that it existed. This community was very much different from the friends I had in person, and the community offered a different (not necessarily better) aspect to my personality which I desperately needed. As much as some of my other friends showed support and curiosity for the fact that I did competitive math to the side, none of them quite understood the toll it could take, and this was very much alleviated by the community who could understand. Eventually, I had finally made the realization that these rowdy math students in my math club were merely crying to be read differently but I just did not have the capacity for emotional maturity to tune into that at the time.

I think being supportive of others, and likewise having others available when you need support, is crucial to succeeding in a hyper competitive environment (or really any environment to be honest), especially in competitive math. Really, there’s no point in winning if you’re unlikeable (hmmm, this may be parallel to my thoughts on certain rich people, perhaps a future blog post on this later?), and there’s not much fun in being really good at math if you’re not going to uplift others in mathematics. So while the general pool of competitive math people can definitely be quite a lot to handle, it gives me great joy that continuously, the best mathematicians are usually also very caring people, who have also experienced similar levels of insecurity and unsure circumstances about what most would consider “standard” social interactions.

There’s so much more I could unpack, but perhaps I will stop here for now. Sometimes my parents had genuinely good nuggets of wisdom, sometimes they needed time to mature their advice, and sometimes they might have completely missed the mark, but that’s totally okay! Above all, I just think it’s really important that I recognize my privilege and that my parents followed their own advice to genuinely want to do good things for me, since there was a time when I believed this was just the bare basics of parenting, but now as I have talked with more people, I realize how lucky I am.

The Thanksgiving spirit probably motivated this post; I wish everyone has a Happy Thanksgiving, hopefully with their loved ones!
