You are NOT in a Chinese Time of your Life - A Conversation and Analysis

March 23, 20267 min readNot-so-investigative journalism

This post is extremely late, but I recently had a conversation at length with one of my many inspirations in life about the phrase “I’m at a very Chinese time in my life.” This statement grew massively popular around the Lunar New Year, and I increasingly questioned why it disturbed me so much. This post is going to be a bit different from my typical post as I will make my best attempt at a comprehensive summary of our conversation in which:

comments written with a green lined indent are her introduced perspectives.

The Conversation#

When I saw a bunch of white girls saying that they were in a very “Chinese time in their lives”, and that they were celebrating the Chinese New Year, I didn’t have anything to say because I’m not Chinese. And then they remembered I was…less than 5 years ago when people accused me of eating dogs, cats, and bats because I was “clearly Chinese” and being Chinese was associated with being dirty and unclean.

At that point in time, being Chinese was looked down upon. People were claiming to be anything other than Chinese. Asians who were not Chinese would intentionally put their country’s flags in their social media bios and profiles to clearly state that they were not Chinese. Some Chinese people even hid the fact that they were Chinese because they were afraid of being discriminated against or receiving hate comments or even death threats.

But it didn’t matter, because if you even so much looked East Asian, then you were automatically Chinese, and your food and ethnic practices were clearly unclean and the reason for the COVID outbreak and other viral diseases. Right…

So, I actually did go through a very Chinese time in my life, and it was extremely unpleasant. I think it’s interesting how white girls can wear jade bracelets and button necklaces, they can wear “Scandinavian” tops and shoes that are clearly appropriated from Indian culture and nowhere near Europe.

And this supposedly makes them trendy, tasteful, humorous, even. But when it’s one worn by an Asian person, it’s whatever, it’s basic, it’s to be expected. But when an Asian person dyes their hair blond, wears a cross necklace, and has lots of white friends, they’re whitewashed and basic. They’re “abandoning” their culture and their roots. They forgot who they are and they’re seeking validation from other people. They’re desperate. Right…

And a white girl can make a kimchi cucumber salad and it will be fun, it’ll be so diverse! They’re just trying something trendy that’s cute and new. They’re going to try some new scary food for the first time…and wait! They actually like it! They’re soooo obsessed! Stoppp…they can’t even…they can’t even…

But when an Asian person makes their own food, it’s whatever. In fact, at one point, it was disgusting, it was smelly. They couldn’t bring it to their school lunch because it was too smelly and too gross. And their peers would point and laugh and tell them it was smelly and that they shouldn’t bring that lunch anymore.

Countless times I remember bringing lunch to school and even in a school with a sizable Asian student body, I still had to hear about how horrible my food must be just because it smelled a little bit. And then they discover the Indian and Bangladeshi food I used to bring for themselves years later. They try it. They’re amazed. They talk wondrously about how delicious it is, citing its plentiful spices and embracing fragrance. Right…

And a white girl can speak Chinese or Korean on the internet and they’ll be praised. They can build an entire page and following off of learning a new language and they’ll be lauded for it. But an Asian person speaking more than one language is just whatever. Again, it’s merely to be expected.

And God forbid an Asian girl struggles to speak her own native language. Once again, she’s whitewashed and basic. She’s clearly not a “true Asian”.

The Analysis#

Maybe I’m too woke, but I want us to really think about what “Being in a Chinese time of our lives” really implies. Something I find interesting about this recent semi-ironic fetishization of Chinese culture is that it went from centering the culture itself to centering the non-Chinese people who engage with it.

We went from jokes about the “Chinese century” where the focus is clearly on the juxtaposition of China and the United States as political entities, to jokes where people say “Chinese food is just food” to them, which despite playing on the normalization of Chinese culture in the United States, actually works to other it.

The following quote is from Thesis 4 of Jeffrey Cohen’s Monster Theory, a literary analysis on the role that monsters play in our media (a super insightful reading I was assigned by Professor Chesa Caparas three years ago!). In his words,

The monster is difference made flesh, come to dwell among us. In its function as dialectical Other or third-term supplement, the monster is an incorporation of the Outside, the Beyond…The exaggeration of cultural difference into monstrous aberration is enough.

Cohen means this to say that monsters in media often embody the fears of the societies that created them. This is in parallel to Muslims in French media, who are often portrayed as monstrous, both literally and metaphorically, as a reflection of French Islamophobia.

But here, I wish to flip this thesis upside down on its head. I want us to think about the last part of the quote: “the exaggeration of cultural difference into monstrous aberration”. The whole premise of “I’m so Chinese that Chinese food is just food to me” is the idea that it’s unnatural, monstrous even, for a white American to view Chinese food as being deracialized. The joke acknowledges Chinese food as being foreign and then places the white American in that foreign space by turning them “so Chinese”. The white American is being othered from whiteness via their proximity to “Chinese-ness”. They are the exaggeration of cultural difference into monstrous aberration.

And this is only made clearer when we consider that the joke simply doesn’t work the opposite way around! Imagine a Chinese American saying “I’m so Italian that Italian food is just normal food to me”. It simply doesn’t work because in America, European food genuinely is “just food”. The humor isn’t derived from the novelty of engaging with a foreign culture; it’s derived from the juxtaposition of what Cohen denotes as “the within” (in this case, white Americans) engaging with what Cohen calls the “monstrous difference” (in this case, Chinese culture).

It’s absolutely worthwhile to examine why we’re all at a very Chinese time in our lives. Because, what’s next? What’s the next culture in the popular zeitgeist? Are Chinese people truly appreciated beyond the country’s products? (i.e. the iconic food dishes and drinks, songs getting popular almost exclusively through memes, etc.) Just interacting with the products of Chinese culture does not go far enough to be considered appreciation. For example, do you bother to understand the people? Do you understand the role that their culture plays in this melting pot of a society? And this also applies to people’s perception of a country’s women. This is where it gets really dicey, because these are actual people! The fetishization of East Asian women is immensely disgusting…and these sentiments now go as far as to say that you can now appreciate this culture’s women for their beauty that they have always held, but have experienced hate and shame for in the past.

So what culture is next? Are you waiting for the next culture to be in the spotlight so you can finally start appreciating them? Maybe the hope for every country and culture’s people in the world is to wait until western society finally commodifies their products so that their beauty can also be recognized.

Or rather, should everyone put in the work to dismantle racial biases that you currently still have and appreciate and really get to know the cultures other than your own, through examination, immersion, appreciation, and understanding what issues actually plague that culture? Because at the end of the day, these cultures have existed for centuries, if not millennia. They’ve existed long before you decided you wanted to appreciate them.

The extended comparison that I wish to draw upon here is between these jokes and anti-miscegenation rhetoric. During the Jim Crow era, a white person who dated outside of their race was seen as being “less white” and “more colored” due to their proximity to “blackness”. In the same way, a white person who say, eats Chinese food is “less white” and “more Chinese” due to their proximity to Chinese culture. This paints Chinese culture as something that is inherently incompatible with whiteness or American-ness. The only way you would engage with Chinese culture is if you were Chinese yourself. And so, in an effort to promote the integration of Chinese culture into American life, I think these jokes are actually doing the opposite.
